# Story grammar, clips and editing

## The reusable causal structure

The references explain a problem by repeatedly improving a visible physical model. Each new sentence changes one understandable state. The first reference has a long chain of material failures/fixes; the second follows a drainage route, then adds reliability features. Neither depends on a human character, a dialogue exchange or an unrelated montage.

| Beat role | What the viewer learns | What the picture does |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | A familiar thing has a surprising problem/result | Establish the entire model, show short headline |
| Basic attempt | The simplest plausible setup | Assemble/activate a simple version |
| Failure | Why that setup is insufficient | Crack, flood, sag, jam, block or otherwise demonstrate the result |
| Fix | Introduce a part with a concrete function | Reveal/install the part; label it briefly |
| Limitation | The fix solves only part of the problem | Keep that part visible and show the next failure |
| Better mechanism | How the improved version works | Follow the actual force/flow/path through the model |
| Test | Whether it holds under the problem condition | Re-run a recognizable stress/rain/load |
| Payoff | Resolve the original hook | Return to a familiar view and show the final state |

For a new subject, the mechanism can be mechanical, spatial, biological, logistical or social. If the subject has no literal hidden layer, use a spatial metaphor that remains coherent. Do not claim an invented mechanism is factual. For fiction, label it as fiction in the script; for factual content, verify claims before visualizing them.

## Clip construction

Treat a clip as a **continuous readable beat**, not a fixed duration copied to every topic. The longer reference keeps the same house and changes view/state; the shorter one keeps the same modern house and water route. Camera position is a way of explaining a new property, not filler motion between disconnected AI-generated images.

Most new causal phrases occupy roughly 1–3 seconds. A change can happen over a small part of that interval, followed by time to read the result. Let object motion, labels and camera moves take turns. Avoid moving all three aggressively while a caption also bounces.

The complete manually assigned beat ranges are in `../measurements/beat_timeline.json`. They are semantic segments, not a claim of a hard cut at every boundary. Each of all 3,294 frames is mapped to a segment in `frame_review_index.csv`.

## New script writing targets

Use 4.2–4.4 words/second including pauses as a first budget, then fit to the actual voice. A 50-second film might use roughly 205–215 words with a short ending hold. Do not compress a 280-word narration into a 17-second film; shorten the causal chain if a shorter duration is requested.

Write short concrete clauses. Name the object once, show what it does, then use the result to introduce the next beat. Words like “but” and “so” are natural turning points, not a formula to repeat every sentence. End with an explanation that answers the hook, rather than a generic “like and subscribe” outro unless the user requests one.

## Transitions and continuity

Use continuous camera moves to related views. A change in story state can use a short dissolve/opacity reveal or a clean purposeful cut when necessary. Do not add a transition effect between every sentence. Intentional flashes in the drainage video are storm effects; they do not license arbitrary white flashes throughout a new story.

Match positions, material boundaries, light direction and object count through every transition. Keep typography screen-stable and labels world-anchored. Avoid a geometry swap that moves the house or changes the tree species halfway through a repair. Finish with enough frames for the last cause-and-effect relationship to register.
